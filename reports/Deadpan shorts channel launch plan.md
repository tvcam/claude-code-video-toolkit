# Say Less, Pause on Purpose, Post Daily

A solo creator can launch this channel for $0 with a stack this repo already half-provides: **Kokoro-82M** (Apache-2.0, runs locally) or the repo's **Qwen3-TTS 1.7B** for the voice, **Remotion plus rough.js line art** in a new 1080×1920 template, **local Whisper** for captions, an **ACE-Step v1.5** bed from the free acemusic key, and the native **YouTube Studio and TikTok web schedulers**. The conflict over pace resolves in favour of **few words, a normal speaking rate and deliberate gaps**: write about **70–95 words for a 30–40 s video**, let the voice speak at roughly 165–175 wpm inside sentences, and insert beats of 0.45–0.9 s sized by function (never over 1.0 s). That lands at **about 140–150 wpm overall**, which is both the slowest pace any measured narrated winner used and the top of the narrator research's 130–140 band. First-hand measurements of 15 TikToks set the editing rules: speak at frame 0, get 8–10 words out in the first 3 s, change the drawing every 2–4 s over constant idle motion so no frame holds past 4.5 s, keep a quiet bed about 20 dB under the voice, and end on a dry tag with no spoken call to action. Money is a long game. A channel starting now faces YouTube's **1 February 2027 entry bar** (1,000 subscribers plus 20M qualified Shorts views in 90 days, or 8,000 watch hours in a year), and TikTok's Creator Rewards pays only for videos **longer than one minute**, so month one is about reach, consistency and learning, with hundreds to low thousands of views per video as the realistic norm. Because 13–17-year-olds are in the audience, hooks must be honest curiosity gaps closed inside the same video, with no part-2 bait, fake urgency or fear framing, topics skewed toward 16–29, and every upload marked "not made for kids". The plan below spends four days building and testing, then posts one video a day from **Sunday 22 November**, and supplies ten complete scripts and a 30-video calendar drawn from verified sources. Several platform rules were reported only by press or blogs because the official help pages were blocked during research; the final section lists what to confirm before launch.

## A zero-dollar stack works, but the repo is missing four pieces

The voice decision is mostly a licensing decision. **Kokoro-82M** is the cleanest free default: its repository describes Apache-licensed weights, it runs on a CPU, outputs 24 kHz audio and exposes a `speed` parameter ([hexgrad/kokoro](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro)). One 2026 deployment guide mentions CC BY-NC-SA in an unclear context, so check the individual voice pack on the model card before monetizing ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/deploy-open-source-tts-gpu-cloud-2026/)). **Qwen3-TTS** is also Apache-2.0 and takes natural-language instructions for tone and speaking rate ([QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS)). It is already wired into the repo through `tools/qwen3_tts.py` and `voiceover.py --provider qwen3`, and both the Modal and RunPod images download the **1.7B CustomVoice** model ([docker/modal-qwen3-tts/app.py](/home/user/claude-code-video-toolkit/docker/modal-qwen3-tts/app.py)). That matters because the 0.6B CustomVoice model ignores `instruct` ([Qwen3-TTS FAQ](https://www.mintlify.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/resources/faq)). Modal's Starter plan includes **$30 a month of compute credit** ([MorphLLM](https://www.morphllm.com/modal-pricing)); at about $1.10 an hour for an A10, that works out to roughly $0.02–0.06 per video by the tool-stack researcher's arithmetic. Two cloud free tiers are legitimate commercial routes with full SSML pause control, but both need a card on file: **Azure Speech F0** gives 0.5M neural characters a month ([Azure pricing](https://azure.microsoft.com/pricing/details/cognitive-services/speech-services)), and Google Cloud TTS gives about 1M Neural2 characters ([texttolab](https://texttolab.com/blog/google-cloud-tts-pricing)). Several free options should be avoided. **edge-tts** is a grey area, because Microsoft staff say no public document grants commercial rights for those voices ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-au/answers/questions/5925556/commercial-use-of-edge-read-aloud-voices-via-edge)). **ElevenLabs Free** is non-commercial, and Starter at $6 a month is the cheapest commercial tier ([BIGVU](https://bigvu.tv/blog/elevenlabs-pricing-2026-plans-credits-commercial-rights-api-costs/)). No commercial grant was found for **TikTok's or CapCut's built-in voices** ([AnySpeech](https://anyspeech.io/blog/tiktok-text-to-speech)). The popular Piper "lessac" voices exclude commercial use ([piper1-gpl LICENSING](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/docs/LICENSING.md)).

Animation is free for this creator. Remotion's license covers individuals and for-profit companies with up to three employees ([Remotion LICENSE.md](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). The repo already pins `@remotion/paths`, whose `evolvePath()` draws an SVG stroke from invisible to complete, the "line being drawn" effect ([Remotion docs](https://remotion.dev/docs/paths/evolve-path)). What it lacks is a sketchy renderer. **rough.js** is MIT-licensed, under 9 kB, and draws shapes and SVG paths "with a sketchy, hand-drawn look" ([rough-stuff/rough](https://github.com/rough-stuff/rough)), which is the cheapest route to "not too perfect". Re-seeding its jitter every few frames gives a "boiling line" hand-drawn feel; that is a known animation technique, not a sourced claim. Pair it with zero-attribution art: **Open Peeps** (CC0), **unDraw**, **Tabler Icons** (MIT) and **Lucide** (ISC) ([Toolfolio](https://toolfolio.com/articles/free-open-source-illustrations-library); [Tabler](https://github.com/tabler/tabler-icons); [Lucide](https://github.com/lucide-icons/lucide)). ## Calm comes from fewer words and deliberate gaps, not a slowed voice

### The pace evidence, reconciled into one target

The two bodies of evidence disagree on numbers but agree on mechanism. The narrator-voice research shows comprehension is not the constraint: listeners understood speech at **270 wpm** and only dropped sharply at 315 ([Kuperman et al., McMaster](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/2127220)), and playback up to 2x cost little comprehension ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/01/220111153637.htm)). Its only direct penalty for slowness is credibility at around **100 wpm or below**, a classic 1976 result known only second-hand ([Miller et al. via Typeset](https://typeset.io/pdf/speed-of-speech-and-persuasion-48bx24hl8s.pdf)). From that it proposed 130–140 wpm overall, with near-normal speed inside phrases and the slowness carried by gaps. The first-hand teardown found **no successful narrated faceless video below about 140 wpm**. The slowest were Zack D. Films' femur-surgery short at **141 wpm** ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@zackdfilms92/video/7315249587311201582)) and SimpliHow's wisdom-teeth short at **152 wpm** with 3.8M views ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@simplihowww/video/7511853084750941470)). The calm 3D explainers ran 141–192 wpm. The deadpan stick-figure clips ran **210–245 wpm** ([Casually Explained](https://www.tiktok.com/@casuallyexplained/video/7576414392284237063)). No measured pause exceeded 0.96 s ([MinuteEarth](https://www.tiktok.com/@minuteearth/video/7636354677780614414)). Dividing those two slowest winners' rates by their measured speaking share gives an articulation rate of roughly **160–175 wpm inside sentences**. That is my arithmetic from the teardown's pause statistics, not a direct measurement.

The two positions converge on one recommendation. **Keep the words few and the sentences short, let the voice speak at a normal rate, and put all of the calm into the gaps.** In practice that means about 165–175 wpm inside sentences, with the engine at or near its default speed and never slowed below 0.9. Add **function-sized beats** that take about 15–18% of runtime. The result is an overall rate of **140–150 wpm, or about 2.4 words per second of finished video**. This respects the user's "slow" intent in every way listeners can hear. Words per second match the slowest measured winner and run about 40% below the deadpan channels. Sentences average about seven words, there are 7–9 audible beats in 30–40 s, and the closing line is spoken slowest. What the recommendation refuses is the one move with no measured success: stretching syllables with the engine's speed control, which the narrator research warns makes TTS sound slurred or robotic. The toolkit's existing budget of 2.5 words per second already sits at the top of this range ([CLAUDE.md](/home/user/claude-code-video-toolkit/CLAUDE.md)).

| Setting | Recommendation (v1) | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Overall rate (words ÷ runtime) | **140–150 wpm** (≈2.35–2.5 words/s) | Floor of measured winners, 141–152 wpm; above the ≤100 wpm credibility penalty |
| Rate inside sentences | ≈165–175 wpm; engine speed ≈1.0 (0.95–1.1), never below 0.9 | Derived articulation of the slowest winners; slowed TTS sounds slurred |
| Hook line | ≤10 words, finished by ≈3.2 s (≈190 wpm, the densest line) | Median 11 words in the first 3 s (range 6–17) |
| Sentence length | ≤10 words, one idea and one visual per sentence | Script-craft guidance |
| Beats (silence inserted in post) | 0.45 s at sentence ends; 0.65 s between How steps; 0.8 s after the hook; 0.9 s before the solution or tag; **never over 1.0 s** | Measured beats of 0.41–0.52 s mean and 0.72–0.96 s max |
| Beat density | ≈14–18 beats ≥0.25 s per minute | Measured 12.5–17.9 (calm/doodle), up to 31 (deadpan) |
| Closing tag | Slowest and lowest line, ≈150 wpm | Punchlines are delivered lower and slower ([Pickering et al.](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/prosodic-markers-of-saliency-in-humorous-narratives/)) |
| Word budget | Runtime × 2.4: 30 s ≈ 72, 35 s ≈ 84, 40 s ≈ 96, 45 s ≈ 108, 55 s ≈ 132 | Arithmetic |
| Default runtime | **28–40 s**; 45–55 s only when a How needs four steps | Measured winners ran 21–41 s; 16–30 s had the highest median views in a 16,537-Short study ([OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/best-youtube-shorts-length)) |

### How to test the pace before and after launch

Run the test in three stages. Stage one happens before launch: render one script in three delivery arms with the shortlisted voices. **Arm S ("slow")** runs about 130–135 wpm overall, with engine speed ≈0.9 and beats up to 1.2 s, which is the narrator research's vision. **Arm C ("calm", recommended)** runs 140–150 wpm as specified above. **Arm B ("brisk calm")** runs about 165–175 wpm, with speed ≈1.1 and beats shrunk to 0.3–0.5 s, matching the measured 3D-explainer median. Show the three to a blind panel of 15–30 people aged 13–29, following parental-consent norms for minors. They rate each arm 1–7 on "calm", "slow/boring", "clear", "funny/dry" and "would keep watching". Stage two is weeks three and four on the platforms. With the voice locked, alternate arms C and B across 14 different videos matched by pillar, seven each. Compare the medians of YouTube's "viewed vs swiped away" metric, average percentage viewed, TikTok full-watch rate, and follows per 1,000 views. Switch to B only if it beats C by **at least five points on stayed-to-watch and on average percentage viewed in at least five of seven pairs**. Otherwise keep C, because it fits the brand and sits inside the evidence. Stage three runs in month two, and only if the panel rated arm S as calmer and no more boring than C. Then put S against C on the platforms, deliberately probing the slow end that no measured winner occupies. Measure every render instead of trusting settings. Words ÷ duration gives overall wpm. `ffmpeg -af silencedetect=noise=-35dB:d=0.25` on the dry voice stem lists the beats, and faster-whisper `base.en` word timestamps reproduce the teardown's own method. TTS engines drift: the toolkit notes ElevenLabs can turn a 50 s script into 40–45 s ([CLAUDE.md](/home/user/claude-code-video-toolkit/CLAUDE.md)). That drift is why the pauses belong in post rather than in the engine.

### Voice style guide: "the Very Calm Narrator"

The persona is **polite, unhurried, mildly weary about the problem and never about the viewer**. It is fully committed to every word and never winks. The research explains why that distinction matters. No experiment shows flat delivery makes a line funnier. Listeners read irony mainly from context and incongruity ([Bryant & Fox Tree](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/bryant-foxtree-isthereanironictoneofvoice-2005)). They also detect a *bored* narrator from voice alone and are less motivated by one ([Lawson et al. 2021](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Lawson2021EdTechResDev.pdf)). Deadpan therefore works when the script and the drawing carry the absurdity and the voice stays calm and competent. It fails when the voice sounds disinterested or the content is dull. Use a **low-to-mid pitch with a narrow but not flat range**, falling declarative endings even on absurd lines, normal word stress and no "smiling voice". Create emphasis by isolation, through a short sentence or a beat before the word, rather than by pitch spikes. Put the key word last in its sentence, and use one-word sentences ("No." "Anyway.") as beats. Give each joke its own sentence and keep it *beside* the instruction, never inside it. Classroom experiments found that humor built into the explanation can reduce learning, while humor adjacent to it helps ([Bolkan et al. via NCA](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/using-humor-classroom-beneficial-or-detrimental-student-learning/)).

Comedic timing needs one more reconciliation. Acoustic studies of joke-telling found punchlines marked by **lower pitch and slower rate, not by a preceding pause** ([Pickering et al.](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/prosodic-markers-of-saliency-in-humorous-narratives/); [Attardo & Pickering](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/HUMR.2011.015/pdf?lang=en)). In the measured shorts, though, the longest beats did sit right before the turn: 0.62 s before ZDF's absurd watermelon finale and 0.84 s before Casually Explained's tag ([ZDF](https://www.tiktok.com/@zackdfilms92/video/7708059129335598350); [Casually Explained](https://www.tiktok.com/@casuallyexplained/video/7576414392284237063)). Do both. Put a 0.9 s beat before the tag, deliver the tag as the slowest, lowest line, and test a 0.45 s pre-tag beat in month two. Pauses at meaningful boundaries also help viewers remember the word that follows ([MacGregor et al.](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/listening-to-the-sound-of-silence-disfluent-silent-pauses-in-spee/)), so place a beat before each step number and before the payload noun. Never place one at random.

The synthetic voice itself is not the main risk. Modern TTS matched a human voice on credibility and learning ([Craig & Schroeder via ASU](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/how-we-trust-perceive-and-learn-from-virtual-humans-the-influence/)), and listeners labelled AI voices correctly only about **60%** of the time ([Barrington et al., Berkeley](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/research/publications/2025/people-are-poorly-equipped-detect-ai-powered-voice-clones)). The risk is **perceived AI-ness**. In a survey of 3,000 US adults, trust fell by nearly half when people *believed* content was AI-made ([PPC Land on Raptive](https://ppc.land/raptive-study-shows-ai-content-cuts-reader-trust-by-half/)). In another, "unnatural voices" were the giveaway for **55%** of people who suspected AI ([Business Wire on Animoto](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260121875037/en)). The defence is to make the voice an obviously designed character: avoid the most-used stock voices and default settings, time beats to visual events so they read as authored, and keep the persona in the writing. Note in the bio, not the videos, that the channel is "narrated by a synthetic voice, written by a human". TikTok's "Jessie" voice shows a recognisable synthetic-sounding voice can become a signature that creators use ironically ([Globe and Mail](https://arc-dev.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-tiktok-talk-voice-tiktalk)). For a US/global audience, a standard North American or neutral international accent is the low-risk default. A standard-accented voice beat a foreign-accented one for learning in Mayer's study, a single and confound-prone result ([Gunadarma record](https://library.gunadarma.ac.id/journal/social-cues-in-multimedia-learning-role-of-speakers-voice)). No reliable evidence exists on narrator gender or age for this audience, so test across them.

To choose the voice, shortlist four to six candidates across engines and genders. Render the same script with identical animation and the arm-C pause map, then run the blind panel described above and add two more ratings: "sounds annoying-AI" and "bored-sounding". Drop any voice that scores high on either and keep the top two. On the platforms, alternate those two across videos #1–#14 (odd numbers voice A, even numbers voice B). Pick the voice that wins stayed-to-watch and follows per 1,000 views by a clear margin. On a tie, take the one the panel found funnier. Then **lock the voice and its settings for at least 30–50 videos** in `brands/<channel>/voice.json`. The repo's brand profile already has a `qwen3` block for speaker, tone and instruct; add a `kokoro` block. Changing the voice later effectively restarts recognition. That is an inference, since no data on voice consistency and follow rate exists.

## First-hand measurements rewrite the editing spec for a 2D deadpan short

The first-hand teardown measured 15 public TikToks, using ffmpeg scene detection, EBU R128 loudness, silence detection and faster-whisper word timestamps, and it takes precedence over the earlier dataset pass. The dataset pass itself holds up: the same Low Poly short measured first-hand on TikTok gave **206 wpm, a first cut at 1.2 s and 4.64 hard cuts per 10 s**, against 206 wpm, 1.2 s and 4.6 from the earlier YouTube data ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@lowpolyshorts/video/7606648171032317197); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/0U6OcfxYZ7U)). No YouTube file could be watched directly, because YouTube demanded sign-in, so all YouTube-specific delivery remains unverified. Three corrections matter for this channel. First, "calm ≈ 171 wpm" holds only for 3D explainers; the deadpan and doodle styles ran faster, at 207–245 wpm. Second, **loop endings were 0 of 13**, while punchline or dry-tag endings were 8 of 13. Third, the old rules of "always moving at frame 0" and "no silence over 0.4 s" fail for hand-drawn styles. Casually Explained's stick-figure clips are **97% frozen frames, with holds up to 4.8 s**, and still reach 0.5–2.4M views. They get away with it because almost every sentence is a joke from an established persona ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@casuallyexplained/video/7583651459095465223)). A new channel cannot borrow that licence, so the spec adds idle motion while allowing the drawing itself to hold.

The hook evidence is the most consistent of all. Every narrated video spoke its first word within **0.1 s**, and the median was **11 words in the first 3 s**. "If…" was the most common opener, used in six of 13. The contrarian follow-up worked in SimpliHow's 12.4M-view headache short: "If you have a headache, don't try to take pain relievers…" ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@simplihowww/video/7445347531694837022)). The misconception turn worked in Low Poly's "you might think… But" ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@lowpolyshorts/video/7612238436992322829)). None of the 12 fully transcribed closings contained a follow or subscribe request. Bedded explainers (ZDF, SimpliHow, MinuteEarth) kept music **14–22 dB under the voice**. Casually Explained used no music at all, with near-silent pauses, which is what makes it read as dry deadpan. Successful files ranged from −9 to −23 LUFS because TikTok does not normalise loudness, and ZDF's hot masters showed true-peak overs above 0 dBTP after encoding. Mastering at −14 LUFS with −1 dBTP true peak is the safe target.

| Element | Spec for this channel | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| First word | 0.0–0.1 s; no greeting, logo or fade-in | 12/12 narrated videos |
| Hook | 8–10 words by ≈3 s, using "you" or an imperative; forms: "If you [situation], [contrarian instruction]"; "[Do X] like this"; "You might think [X]. But…"; a direct question | Median 11 words, range 6–17; "If…" in 6/13 |
| Frame 0 | Subject visible and already moving (hand mid-action, object falling, line drawing itself) | 3D and live samples moved by 0.1 s |
| First visual event | Pop-on, smash or zoom accent by 1.0–2.0 s | Laser 0.5 s, tumour 1.0 s, smash 2.0 s |
| Visual rhythm | One new drawn element, pose or label per sentence (every 2–4 s); constant idle motion (blink, breathing bob, 2–5% camera push); **no unchanged frame over 4.5 s**; hold still through the pre-tag beat, then reveal on the punchword | 2D winners 94–97% frozen, holds ≤4.8 s |
| Cuts | 0–1 hard cuts per 10 s | ZDF, SimpliHow, CE, MinuteEarth: 0–0.9 |
| Captions | 1–3 words, sentence case, white heavy rounded sans, soft shadow or 3–4 px outline; text block ≈60–70 px tall (font ≈72–84 px); centre **y ≈1,320–1,440 px**, x ≈90–930 px; swap every 0.5–1.0 s; cut on and off with no bounce; optional single keyword accent box | ZDF y≈1,495; SimpliHow 1,410–1,460; MinuteEarth red keyword box |
| Series header | Small persistent "BORING BUT USEFUL #N" at y ≈420–560 px | Casually Explained's header pattern |
| Safe zones | Keep text above y ≈1,480 and below y ≈220; ≥150 px from the right rail; validate once in both apps | TikTok bottom UI ≈250–370 px ([AdMake](https://www.admakeai.com/blog/vertical-video-dimensions-2026)); Shorts ≈380 px ([PostEverywhere](https://posteverywhere.ai/blog/youtube-shorts-aspect-ratio)); vendor approximations |
| Sound | Default: quiet bed ≈18–22 dB under the voice (voice −14 LUFS, bed ≈−34 LUFS short-term); test dry (no music) for myth episodes in month two; sparse soft diegetic SFX (pop, pencil scratch), no meme SFX | Bed levels measured; SFX not measured |
| Master | −14 LUFS integrated, ≤−1 dBTP, LRA ≤4 LU | Winners −9 to −23 LUFS; ZDF peak overs |
| Ending | Dry one-line tag or a clean result image; no spoken CTA; cut 0.4–0.9 s after the last word; no engineered loop | Dry tag/punchline 8/13; loops 0/13; CTAs 0/12 |
| Length | 28–40 s default | Narrated winners 21–41 s |

The calm and high-energy samples differ in cut rate, not in speed to premise. MrBeast's 90.2M-view short is a single take with reaction pauses of up to 3.5 s ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@mrbeast/video/7670566355153833246)), while Alan Chikin Chow cuts 11.4 times per 10 s ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@alanchikinchow/video/7621178509800508703)). Both start talking or moving at frame zero. A calm channel should borrow the front-loading: instant speech, a moving first frame, the answer stated early, and a dry tag. It should leave behind the cut rate, the shouting and the meme sounds. Two sampling limits stay open. Each style had only one to three samples, and no deadpan channel *documented* as using an AI voice reached 1M views; the two TTS-like samples were identified by inference. Retention data is not public, so "works" means views.

## Honest curiosity, closure and no bait: the ethical line for a teen-inclusive feed

The audience is heavy-using. Among US teens, **76% use YouTube and 61% use TikTok daily, and 21% say they are on TikTok "almost constantly"**, up from 16% in 2022 ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/12/09/teens-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-2025/)). The research on harm is real but narrower than the headlines. A meta-analysis of 71 studies found heavier short-video use associated with poorer attention (r = −.38) and inhibitory control (r = −.41) ([PsyPost on Nguyen et al. 2025](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/)). The data were mostly cross-sectional, the cognitive subset came from China, and the associations tracked *compulsive* use more than minutes watched ([Reason](https://reason.com/2025/12/31/study-short-form-video-isnt-rotting-your-brain)). Estimates of "addiction" range from about 4% to 18% depending on the scale and sample; a 2025 study found only 9% of US college TikTok users at risk ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27053-2)). The "8-second, shorter-than-a-goldfish" attention span is a myth traced to a marketing report ([WARC](https://www.warc.com/NewsAndOpinion/opinion/The_goldfish_myth/2806)). The defensible claim is that viewers *decide* fast in a swipe feed, not that they cannot attend. TikTok's internal research, surfaced in Kentucky's lawsuit, reportedly found habit formation after about 260 videos ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5150594)). A creator cannot control any viewer's total time. A creator can avoid design whose purpose is to extend compulsive viewing.

The useful finding is that the most evidence-backed levers are also the ethical ones. **Curiosity about a question improves memory for the answer**, even a week or two later ([Kang et al. 2009](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf); [Gruber et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)), so an honest information gap closed in the same video serves engagement and learning together. **Open loops do not work the way marketers claim**: a 2025 meta-analysis found no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks, only a robust urge to *resume* them ([Ghibellini & Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)), and that urge is exactly what part-2 bait exploits. **Endings dominate memory**, because retrospective ratings track the peak and the end far more than duration ([Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)), a result a 2022 meta-analysis supported ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)); a crisp solution plus a dry closing line beats padding. **Signaling with on-screen keywords improves retention** (g = 0.53 across about 100 studies) ([ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling)), which justifies the step counters and keyword overlays. **Fragmented clip streams impair memory** compared with coherent content ([Chiossi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03714v1); [ScienceBlog on Wei et al.](https://scienceblog.com/t-short-form-videos-may-make-newly-learned-information-harder-to-remember-but-the-experiment-tested-fragmented-travel-clips-rather-than-every-brief-lesson-or-every-persons-scrolling-habit/)), so one topic per video keeps the channel on the right side of that evidence. And across 6.9M edX sessions, **hand-drawn tutorials engaged learners more than slides** ([Guo et al.](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)), which loosely supports drawn animation over text cards.

The one real tension is sharing. High-arousal emotions and practical value drive shares, while low-arousal content shares less ([Berger & Milkman](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)). A calm voice should therefore carry *surprising, useful* content (awe, amusement, "wait, that's been there the whole time?"), never the anger and anxiety routes that also spread.

Gen Z's relationship with the feed is ambivalent, and the brand can live inside that ambivalence. "Brain rot" was Oxford's 2024 Word of the Year after its usage rose 230% in a year ([Oxford University Press](https://corp.oup.com/news/brain-rot-named-oxford-word-of-the-year-2024/)). Think with Google reported that 83% of Gen Z had used YouTube to watch soothing content, though that is a 2022 Ipsos figure ([Think with Google](https://business.google.com/ca-en/think/consumer-insights/gen-z-soothing-videos/)). TikTok's 2026 forecast names "Reali-TEA", a shift from polished fantasy toward shared truth, humour and emotional honesty ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-shares-2026-trend-predictions-for-marketers/809651/)). Yet daily use is not falling in Pew's data, and YouTube's own 2025 trends report celebrated loud, referential "creative maximalism" ([ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-study-reveals-creative-maximalism-trend-reshaping-content-for-generation-z/)). Calm is therefore a contrast *inside* short-form, not an exit from it, and the video still has to win the first second. Absurdist, internet-native humour is often called Gen Z's register, but the only experiment found used a small Ohio high-school sample ([JSR](https://www.jsr.org/hs/index.php/path/article/download/2011/917/11893)), and in-jokes such as "6-7" age within months. Deadpan understatement is the evergreen version: a flat voice, a slightly absurd drawing and genuinely useful stakes, with the joke living in the gap between low energy and high value.

The platforms are already adding friction for teens. TikTok gives every under-18 account a default 60-minute daily limit and, since March 2025, interrupts under-16s after 10 pm ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/new-ways-we-are-supporting-parents-and-helping-teens-build-balanced-digital-habits?lang=en)). YouTube turns on Take a Break and bedtime reminders by default for under-18s ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/teen-wellbeing-on-youtube-europe/)). Regulators are moving against design rather than creators. The European Commission preliminarily found TikTok's infinite scroll, autoplay and recommender in breach of the DSA in February 2026 ([Euronews](https://euronews.com/2026/02/06/tiktoks-addictive-design-breaches-eu-law-commission-says)). New York's SAFE for Kids rules switch off personalized feeds for unconsented minors from **25 January 2027** ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-governor-hochul-release-final-safe-kids-act-rules)). A Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube negligent in platform design in March 2026 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/25/meta-youtube-los-angeles-california-verdict.html)). The practical consequence is that content viewers seek out survives a take-a-break prompt better than content that relies on uninterrupted scrolling. That makes follows, search and natural closure more valuable than cliffhangers.

| Use | Avoid (with an audience that includes 13–17-year-olds) |
|---|---|
| An honest gap ("There's an arrow on your dashboard. It's been pointing at the answer this whole time"), answered in this video | Fake cliffhangers, "wait for the end", part-2 splits of content that fits in one video |
| The answer stated by about the first third of runtime; How and demo after | Titles, first frames or covers the video doesn't deliver, which YouTube's misleading-metadata policy prohibits ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973)) |
| Specific, checkable stakes ($, minutes, a mistake avoided) | Fear, outrage or shame hooks ("you've been poisoning yourself"); appearance or status insecurity |
| A dry tag and a stopping cue ("Go check your smoke alarm") | Fake urgency and FOMO ("before this gets deleted", "only 1% know"); FOMO correlates with problematic use, r ≈ .49 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-026-01762-9)) |
| No spoken CTA (0 of 12 measured closings had one); at most a low-pressure line in the caption or a pinned comment inviting topic requests | "Like if…", "comment X", "follow for part 2", which count as engagement manipulation ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973)) |
| Every fact sourced, with the source shown | Unverified AI-generated "facts"; sensory-overload cutting; late-night re-engagement scheduling |

Each script should pass a five-question test before it is rendered. Is every promise in the title, cover and first two seconds fully delivered in *this* video? Would a viewer who rewatched feel informed rather than tricked? Does the hook run on curiosity, usefulness or surprise instead of fear, anger, shame or FOMO? Does the ending give closure and a stopping point? Is every fact verified, and is AI use disclosed where a platform requires it? On the channel and in any write-up, say "compulsive use" rather than "addiction", and "reward learning" rather than "dopamine hits". The research treats both of the popular terms as overstated ([News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/health/Is-Dopamine-Detoxing-Actually-Backed-by-Science.aspx)).

## Five pillars, one wrapper, and a skeleton that never looks templated

The proven faceless-animation lanes are crowded. Zack D. Films has about **29M subscribers** with 3D odd-fact shorts ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/pt/youtube-stats/channel/UCvz84_Q0BbvZThy75mbd-Dg/)), and "recreate Zack D. Films with AI" is now a packaged tutorial ([Snaplama](https://www.snaplama.com/blog/how-to-create-animation-videos-like-zack-d-films-in-2026)). Kapwing's test account found about **21% of the first 500 Shorts served to it were "AI slop"** ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/one-in-five-youtube-shorts-shown-to-new-users-is-ai-generated-slop-study-finds/)). The closest tonal model is Casually Explained, a deadpan stick-figure channel with about 4.2M subscribers built on roughly 134 videos ([vidpros](https://vidpros.com/top-faceless-youtube-channels/)). It shows that a distinct voice beats volume. The underserved space, a reasoned hypothesis rather than a measured gap, is **dry delivery plus genuinely useful life admin for 16–25-year-olds**, presented calm and low-stimulation. That sits where Gen Z's demand for practical learning on social video meets their skepticism of it. TikTok is Gen Z's top platform for financial education at 59% ([Greenlight](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240405996275/en/Greenlight-Survey-Finds-Financial-Anxiety-Begins-as-Young-as-14-Years-Old)), yet 82% in one vendor survey said short-form finance advice oversimplifies ([WallStreetZen](https://www.wallstreetzen.com/blog/genz-money-social-media-survey/)). Showing the source on screen is the answer to both, and it doubles as an anti-slop signal.

Recognisability comes from repeating the **wrapper** while varying the **content and structure**. That is the right side of YouTube's "inauthentic content" rule, which targets templated videos with little variation rather than consistent branding ([air.io](https://air.io/en/monetization/youtube-monetization-policy-changes-2026-a-complete-dated-timeline)). The wrapper is one recurring mascot (a blank-faced stick figure with a coffee mug, drawable in under five minutes per pose, backed by a reusable pose library of stare, point, hold sign and lie on floor), one font and caption style, a pastel background colour per pillar, the corner "BORING BUT USEFUL #N" badge, a 0.5 s soft sting, and a short sign-off habit that sometimes starts with "Anyway." Keep the mascot adult-coded (mug, tie, tiredness) and avoid nursery palettes, kid voices, toys, songs and "for kids" wording. YouTube's made-for-kids designation would strip comments, notifications and personalized ads ([Tubefilter](https://tubefilter.com/2020/01/07/youtube-disables-more-product-features-kid-oriented-content/)), and simple cartoons on school topics can look child-directed. Comment-reply episodes ("You asked: …") are cheap ideation and visible proof that a person runs the channel.

Every script uses the requested **Hook → Why → What → How → Solution** skeleton, with a dry tag on the end. At the recommended pace, a 30–40 s video divides as follows.

| Beat | Time | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3.2 s, then a 0.8 s beat | 8–10 | Keyword and stake; visual already mid-problem |
| Why | ≈4–11 s | 10–18 | Why it matters to *you*; an optional dry aside after the useful line |
| What | ≈11–15 s | 6–12 | Name the thing in one plain sentence. This is the micro-payoff, inside the first ≈40% |
| How | ≈15–28 s, 0.65 s between steps | 18–30 | Two to three numbered steps with a visible "1/3" counter, one drawing per step |
| Solution | ≈28–33 s, then a 0.9 s beat | 7–14 | The result restated plainly; escalation or safety exit if relevant |
| Tag | Last 2–4 s, slowest line | 3–10 | Dry closer; no CTA |

Vary the surface so the skeleton never looks stamped. Rotate the hook type, move the step count between two and four and the runtime between 25 and 45 s, and let the Why sometimes be a myth correction. Roughly one video in four should open as "Myth / No / Fact" or as a list of three that still maps onto Why–What–How–Solution. Searchability follows the same discipline. TikTok reads spoken audio and burned-in text ([ShortGen](https://shortgen.io/blog/tiktok-seo-2026)), and YouTube treats the Shorts title as a topical signal ([Cut.pro](https://cut.pro/en/blog/shorts-tiktok-seo-2026)). Say the keyword in the first sentence and show it in the frame-1 text. Lead the title with it, as in "How to Freeze Bread (Boring but Useful #14)" in under 60 characters. Put the source link in the description, and use three to five specific hashtags with no #fyp. These rules come from SEO blogs, which agree on direction but not on specifics.

## Thirty posting days: build four, then publish one calm video a day

Today is Wednesday 18 November 2026. The plan spends **Wednesday to Saturday (18–21 November) building and testing**, then treats **Sunday 22 November as Day 1** and posts one video a day through **Monday 21 December (Day 30)**. That timing lands the grease-fire episode two days before Thanksgiving (Thursday 26 November), the unit-price episode on Black Friday (27 November) and the delivery-scam episode on Cyber Monday (30 November). December then runs on finals season, winter heating and holiday travel.

The cadence follows the best available data. Buffer's study of more than 11M TikTok posts found that **posting 2–5 times a week gave up to 17% more views per post than posting once, and 11 or more times gave up to 34% more**, with gains flattening after that ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/creator-growth-playbook/)). Its consistency finding, roughly 450% more engagement per post for creators who posted in at least 20 of 26 weeks, is known only second-hand ([PostEverywhere](https://posteverywhere.ai/blog/how-often-to-post-on-social-media)). YouTube's Shorts lead said "there's nothing in the algorithm that dictates expanded or reduced reach if you post more Shorts", while warning that lots of low-quality clips can hurt ([Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/34020532)). So the target is **one video a day, never more, with a floor of five a week**, produced in weekly batches.

Posting slots are **4:00 pm ET on weekdays and 10:00 am ET on weekends and holidays**. The weekday slot sits after school and class, and overlaps both Sprout Social's Tuesday–Thursday 2–6 pm TikTok window ([Sprout](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/best-time-to-post-on-tiktok/)) and Buffer's Friday 4–7 pm Shorts slots ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/best-time-to-post-on-youtube/)). Those datasets disagree with each other and are dominated by brand accounts. Hold the slots fixed for month one so they do not confound the voice and pace tests, then move to the channel's own "When your viewers are on YouTube" and TikTok follower-activity data. ### Platform rules that shape the plan

**YouTube monetization is a 2027 problem, not a December one.** Today, the fan-funding tier reportedly needs 500 subscribers, 3 uploads in 90 days, and either 3,000 watch hours or 3M Shorts views in 90 days. Ad revenue reportedly needs 1,000 subscribers and either 4,000 watch hours or 10M Shorts views in 90 days ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/monetization/youtube-partner-program-requirements-2026-the-complete-guide)). YouTube announced on 10 August 2026 that from **1 February 2027** new applicants will need **1,000 subscribers plus 8,000 qualified watch hours in 365 days, or 1,000 subscribers plus 20M qualified Shorts views in 90 days**. Partner channels will also need **10M qualified Shorts views per rolling 90 days** to share in the Shorts Creator Pool, and updated terms must be accepted by 31 January 2027 ([NewscastStudio](https://www.newscaststudio.com/2026/08/10/youtube-partner-program-monetization-changes-2027/); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/youtube-cuts-shorts-pay-for-channels-under-10m-views-from-february-2027/); [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-updates-partner-program-terms-shorts-payout-rules/585331/)). Eligibility counts **engaged views**, not the public count that since 31 March 2025 includes every start and replay ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-changes-shorts-view-counts-no-change-to-monetization/543005/)). A channel launching now will not reach 10M engaged views by the end of January, so plan against the 2027 bar. No source confirms whether the 500-subscriber tier survives February 2027. The practical second route is watch hours. From month two, compile the back catalogue into 8–12-minute "boring but useful" long-form videos; vidIQ reports hybrid channels tend to outperform single-format ones ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)).

**TikTok will be a reach platform, not a revenue one.** Creator Rewards consistently appears in creator-tool guides with these requirements: age 18+, 10,000 followers, 100,000 views in the last 30 days, a Personal account, an eligible country and **original videos longer than one minute** ([Postlink](https://postlinkapp.com/blog/tiktok-creator-rewards-program); [Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/creator/post/how-to-join-the-tiktok-creator-rewards-program)). The user's sub-minute format therefore earns nothing there. Once the account passes 10,000 followers, test 61–90 s "deep dive" versions of the proven topics on TikTok only; that is the one place the "under a minute" rule should bend. Guides also list slideshows, text-only videos and minimally edited reuploads as non-qualifying, which is another reason to animate every video bespoke ([SocialBoost Digital](https://socialboostdigital.com/blog/tiktok-creator-rewards-unoriginal-content-flag)).

**AI disclosure is light for this format, and transparency is cheap.** YouTube does not require its altered-or-synthetic disclosure for content that is "clearly unrealistic, animated, includes special effects, or has used generative AI for production assistance" ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/)). A generic, non-impersonating TTS narrator over vector drawings most likely answers "No". Blogs conflict on generic AI voiceovers, though, and report that disclosing does not limit monetization ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/ai-disclosure-rules/)), so disclose whenever a voice resembles a real person or any imagery is realistic. TikTok requires self-labels on *realistic* AI content and has auto-labelled uploads carrying C2PA credentials since May 2024 ([MakeInfluence](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/content-credentials-c2pa-how-platforms-label-ai-assisted-content)). Since November 2025, viewers can also turn down how much AI-generated content they see ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/18/tiktok-now-lets-you-choose-how-much-ai-generated-content-you-want-to-see/)). Check whether any tool in the pipeline embeds C2PA metadata, and keep the bio line in place.

**The real monetization risk is sameness.** On 15 July 2025, YouTube renamed "repetitious content" to **"inauthentic content"**, aimed at mass-produced, templated output. YouTube described the change as minor and said it was not a crackdown on faceless channels ([AOL/TechCrunch](https://www.aol.com/youtube-monetization-policies-battles-ai-154324339.html); [AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/news/2025/7/youtube-updates-its-policy-to-demonetize-inauthentic-mass-produced-ai-generated-content)). The defences are concrete: original sourced scripts, bespoke animation per video, rotating hooks and structures, visible replies to comments, no more than one upload a day, and **never deleting and reposting** an underperformer, which YouTube's Shorts lead said can cause downgrades and spam labels ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-advice-for-shorts-creators-hashtags-algorithm/691835/)). Upload the same clean, watermark-free master natively to each platform; Instagram has long said it avoids recommending Reels that carry another app's watermark ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-including-your-own-logo-on-a-reel-is-ok/730852/)). Blogs claim a July 2026 restructuring that names AI personas giving finance, health or legal advice as at risk ([Faceless.my](https://faceless.my/youtube/youtube-inauthentic-content-policy-faceless-ai-2026/)). That is unverified, but it is the reason money and safety episodes are framed as "what the CFPB/CDC/USFA says", carry their caveat lower-thirds, and stay a minority of uploads.

**"Not made for kids", but built for a teen-inclusive feed.** Videos marked made-for-kids lose comments, notifications and personalized ads ([Tubefilter](https://tubefilter.com/2020/01/07/youtube-disables-more-product-features-kid-oriented-content/)), so set the channel and every upload to "No" and keep the audience signals clearly teen-and-adult. Several rules shape who can watch. TikTok keeps content made by **under-16 creators** out of the For You feed ([TikTok Transparency Center](https://www.tiktok.com/transparency/en/protecting-teens/)), so the account holder must be an adult. Australia has barred under-16s from TikTok and YouTube accounts since 10 December 2025 ([eSafety](https://www.esafety.gov.au/key-issues/esafety-guide/tiktok)). YouTube's US age estimation, live since August 2025, switches off personalized ads for viewers it infers are under 18 ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/youtube-ai-powered-technology-teen-users)). A teen-heavy audience therefore earns less and is shrinking by law in some markets. Skew topics toward 16–29 (first job, first apartment, college) while keeping everything appropriate for 13-year-olds.

**Music: bake an original bed into the master.** Business TikTok accounts are limited to the Commercial Music Library, and personal accounts keep the full library ([TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/how-to-use-the-commercial-music-library)). On YouTube, a Short over one minute with any active Content ID claim is reportedly blocked globally; a reported September 2026 relaxation is unconfirmed ([YouTube Help via search extract](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)). An ACE-Step v1.5 bed mixed into a sub-minute master avoids all of this. Do not register AI tracks in Content ID.

### Weekly scorecard

Expectations should come from the one large dataset available. vidIQ studied **448,413 channels** and found the typical first Short reached **1,362 views after 30 days**. Among channels that posted at least ten Shorts in their first 90 days, **86.5% saw one of their first ten pass 1,000 views within 30 days**; that sample is filtered toward active posters ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). Plan for **100–1,500 views on most videos in month one**, one or two outliers, and tens to low hundreds of followers. Shorts keep accruing views for days to weeks ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/how-long-does-it-take-for-yt-shorts-to-get-views/)), so judge trends and medians, not single videos in their first day. Every "good" threshold below is creator folklore; YouTube publishes no benchmark for stayed-to-watch ([Grow Creator](https://growcreator.pro/blog/viewed-vs-swiped-away-youtube-shorts)).

| Metric (weekly, per platform) | Where | Starting target | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viewed vs swiped away ("stayed to watch") | YouTube Studio, per Short | Rising week over week; ≈60–70% is often cited as strong | Hook quality; voice and pace arms |
| Average percentage viewed / average watch time | YouTube Studio; TikTok Analytics | ≥70–80% on 25–40 s (vendor heuristic); >100% means rewatches | Body pacing, payoff timing |
| TikTok "watched full video" % | TikTok Analytics | Compare to own median | Ending and length |
| Shares + saves per 1,000 views | Both | Compare week over week; useful tips should over-index | Practical-value pillars |
| Follows/subs per 1,000 views | Both | Compare week over week | Whether the series identity converts |
| Engaged views vs views | YouTube Analytics, Advanced Mode | Track the 90-day total | Distance to YPP |
| Comment signals | Both | Count "AI voice" complaints vs voice praise; log topic requests | Voice lock; idea queue |
| Output and time | Own log | 7 of 7 posted (floor 5); minutes per video falling toward ≈60 | Sustainability |

Every Sunday, pull the **three best and three worst** videos by stayed-to-watch and average percentage viewed. Compare their first three seconds frame by frame, rewrite one hook template, move one topic up or down the queue, and log the test arm each video ran under. That loop teaches more than any posting-time tweak.

## Ten scripts ready to record

The ten scripts below cover all five pillars and fill ten of the first thirteen calendar slots (#1–3, #6–7 and #9–13). Each follows **Hook → Why → What → How → Solution**, then a dry tag. Timings were computed for arm C: hook at 3.2 words/s, body at 2.9 words/s (≈174 wpm), the tag at 2.5 words/s as the slowest line, and a 0.6 s tail. They land at **70–91 words, 30–38 s and 140–146 wpm overall**.

`[pause Xs]` marks an exact silence inserted in post between per-sentence TTS clips; it is never typed into the engine. Spoken numbers are spelled out ("nine one one") so the TTS stresses them correctly. "On-screen text" lists overlays only. The burned-in captions carry every spoken word in 1–3-word chunks at y ≈1,320–1,440 px, and "header" means the persistent "BORING BUT USEFUL #N" badge. The mascot is the blank-faced stick figure with a mug. Every visual note keeps idle motion running underneath (blink, breathing bob, slow push), so no frame holds longer than 4.5 s.

After rendering, measure the real duration and beats, then trim or extend the pauses, not the words. Each script cites the sources its facts rest on. Re-open those pages before recording, because several were checked through search extracts.

## Conclusion

The biggest shift in understanding is that "slow" is a property of the script, not of the voice. The research never contradicted the user's wish for calm narration. It contradicted only the mechanism of turning the engine down. A video built from roughly 80 words, seven-word sentences and nine audible beats is designed to feel unhurried at 145 wpm, while a stretched voice at 120 wpm has no measured precedent and risks sounding broken. The same logic generalises. Calm is something the edit *builds*: idle motion instead of cuts, a quiet bed instead of silence, a dry tag instead of a loop. The evidence-backed ethical constraints (close every loop, state the answer early, end with a stopping cue) turn out to be the same moves the measured winners already make, so "anti-brain-rot" can be the brand's honest positioning rather than a sacrifice.

The 2027 monetization rules change what success means for this launch. At 20M qualified Shorts views per 90 days to join and 10M to keep earning from the Shorts pool, and with TikTok paying only for videos over a minute, the first year's asset is not ad revenue. It is a **verified, sourced back catalogue with a recognisable voice**. That catalogue can be compiled into long-form for watch hours, cut into 61–90 s TikTok deep dives once the account passes 10,000 followers, and defended against "AI slop" suspicion because every fact links to an agency or a paper. The month-one decisions are deliberately few and dated: the voice is locked by Sunday 6 December, the pace by Monday 21 December, and dry vs bedded sound is tested in January. Everything else should stay consistent long enough for the data to mean something.
