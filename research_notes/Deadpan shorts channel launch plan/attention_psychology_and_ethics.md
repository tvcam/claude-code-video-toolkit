## 1. How much and how compulsively do Gen Z / young adults use short-form video? (usage, "addiction" prevalence, outcomes, regulation)

### Takeaway
Short-form video use among US teens is near-universal and heavy (about 3 in 4 use YouTube daily and ~6 in 10 use TikTok daily; about 1 in 5 say they are on TikTok or YouTube "almost constantly"). "Addiction" estimates vary widely (roughly 4–18% "at risk", depending on the scale and sample). Links to poorer attention, sleep and mental health are consistent but mostly cross-sectional, so they are correlations, not proven causes. Regulators in the US, EU, UK and Australia now treat infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications and personalized feeds as potentially "addictive design" for minors. Those rules target platforms, not individual creators, but they change how teens reach content.

### Cited Findings

**Usage (US teens, 13–17)**
- Pew Research Center, *Teens, Social Media and AI Chatbots 2025* (US teens 13–17, n=1,458, fielded Sept 25–Oct 9, 2025; published Dec 9, 2025): daily use is YouTube 76%, TikTok 61%, Instagram 55%, Snapchat 46%, Facebook 20%. Pew describes daily use as roughly steady versus earlier years — [Pew 2025 report](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/12/09/teens-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-2025/); [Pew PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/12/PI_2025.12.09_Teens-Social-Media-AI_REPORT.pdf)
- Same Pew survey: about 1 in 5 teens say they use TikTok and YouTube "almost constantly". TikTok "almost constantly" is 21% (up from 16% in 2022). Roughly a third of teens are "almost constantly" on at least one of the five platforms (lead author Michelle Faverio). Boys are more likely than girls to be on YouTube almost constantly (20% vs 13%) — [Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/12/09/teens-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-2025/); [The Star / AP coverage](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/12/10/survey-most-us-teens-use-youtube-and-tiktok-daily-some-almost-constantly). **Conflict:** secondary write-ups put the overall YouTube "almost constantly" share at 17% in one source and "about 20%" in another. Check the Pew topline before quoting an exact YouTube figure — [Pew topline PDF](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/12/PI_2025.12.09_Teens-Social-Media-AI_TOPLINE.pdf)
- Same Pew survey, by race and ethnicity (from a search extract, so verify against the topline): 35% of Black teens and 23% of Hispanic teens say they are on YouTube almost constantly, versus 8% of White teens — [Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/12/09/teens-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-2025/)
- Ever-use per secondary coverage of the same Pew report: YouTube 92%, TikTok 68%, Instagram 63%, Snapchat 55%. A July 2025 Pew short read reported TikTok 63%, Instagram 61%, Snapchat 55%, so the ever-use numbers differ by survey — [Pew 10 facts (Jul 2025)](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/10/10-facts-about-teens-and-social-media/); [Powers Health summary](https://www.powershealth.org/about-us/newsroom/health-library/2025/12/16/most-us-teens-use-youtube-and-tiktok-daily-pew-finds)
- Gallup / Institute for Family Studies (US teens 13–19, ~1,500–1,591 respondents, published Oct 2023):
  - Teens averaged 4.8 hours/day across seven social platforms, with YouTube at ~1.9 h/day and TikTok at ~1.5 h/day (Instagram 0.9 h). 51% reported 4+ hours/day.
  - Girls averaged 5.3 h vs 4.4 h for boys, and 17-year-olds averaged 5.8 h — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/512576/teens-spend-average-hours-social-media-per-day.aspx)
- Same Gallup/IFS work: teens using social media more than 5 h/day were reported as 60% more likely to express suicidal thoughts or self-harm. The association was found only for YouTube and TikTok. This is correlational and self-reported — [Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/512576/teens-spend-average-hours-social-media-per-day.aspx); [IFS blog](https://ifstudies.org/blog/how-parenting-and-self-control-mediate-the-link-between-social-media-use-and-youth-mental-health)
- US Surgeon General's Advisory (May 2023):
  - Up to 95% of US 13–17-year-olds use social media.
  - Adolescents spending more than 3 h/day on social media had double the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms. This comes from a cited 2019 cohort study of 12–15-year-olds, not new advisory data.
  - The advisory said there is not enough evidence to determine whether social media is sufficiently safe for young people, and acknowledged benefits, e.g. for LGBTQ youth — [News-Medical summary of the advisory](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20230525/United-States-Surgeon-Generale28099s-Advisory-highlights-social-mediae28099s-effects-on-youth-mental-health.aspx); [ABC News4 coverage](https://abcnews4.com/news/nation-world/surgeon-general-fears-social-media-is-an-important-driver-of-youth-mental-health-crisis-teenagers-online-youtube-tiktok-instagram-snapchat-psychology-brain-development-benefits-community-creativity-risks-anxiety-depression)
- Ofcom, *Children and Parents: Media Use and Attitudes Report 2025* (UK, ages 3–17, published 7 May 2025) is the UK benchmark. Secondary extracts gave inconsistent social-media-profile figures (79% vs other numbers), so no specific Ofcom number is reported here — [Ofcom 2025 report page](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-children/children-and-parents-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025)
- Market surveys (lower quality, vendor data):
  - NuVoodoo, Q1 2025 (US, n≈2,900): 92% of Gen Z and 90% of Millennials watch short-form video "frequently" or "sometimes" — [NuVoodoo](https://nuvoodoo.com/2025/04/04/new-data-short-form-video-explodes-in-popularity/)
  - Korean Consumer Insight survey, Aug 2025 (Korean teens, so a non-US population): 62% preferred short-form vs 47% full-length — [Asia Business Daily](https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2025081414144377229)

**"Addiction" / problematic use prevalence (no pooled short-video prevalence meta-analysis was found)**
- Su et al. 2021, *NeuroImage* (Chinese young adults): about 5.9% of TikTok/Douyin users may have significant problematic use, and more symptoms were linked to lower self-control — [PubMed 33951514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951514/)
- *Scientific Reports* (Nature) 2025, "Overestimates of social media addiction are common but costly" (convenience sample of US college TikTok users): only 9% met an at-risk-of-addiction threshold. The authors note this matches a recent meta-analysis estimate of 4–12% "addicted", which was itself largely based on college samples. The paper's title argues that people commonly overestimate their own "addiction" — [Nature Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27053-2)
- Saudi Arabia adults, cross-sectional 2026, n=411, TikTok Addiction Test: 18.5% prevalence, highest among 18–25-year-olds — [BMC Psychology 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-026-05498-y)
- Older latent-profile study of college students (pre-2024, *Addictive Behaviors*): 6.4% "at risk" of TikTok addiction — [Sage review citing it](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/21501319251327303)
- Systematic review 2025 (26 studies, 11,462 participants): 80.19% pooled prevalence of TikTok *use* (85.4% among 18–29). This measures use, not addiction — [Jain et al. 2025, J Primary Care & Community Health](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/21501319251327303)
- An unverified 32-nation meta-analysis figure of 13–25% "problematic social media use" circulates on a stats blog. It refers to social media generally, not short video, and the original was not checked — [ScrollGuard blog (secondary, low quality)](https://scrollguard.app/blog/short-form-video-addiction-statistics)

**Outcomes (attention, wellbeing, sleep) — mostly correlational**
- Nguyen et al. 2025, *Psychological Bulletin* 151(9):1125–1146, "Feeds, feelings, and focus" (71 studies, 98,299 participants):
  - Heavier short-form video (SFV) use was associated with poorer cognition (r = −.34), with the largest associations for attention (r = −.38) and inhibitory control (r = −.41).
  - Most studies were cross-sectional, and few controlled for general social media use — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/); [NYU IFP abstract listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2025/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000498/)
- Critique of Nguyen et al.:
  - The cognitive analysis relied on a subset of 14 studies, all from China.
  - Cognitive associations were tied more to compulsive or "addictive" use intensity than to time spent — [Reason, Dec 2025](https://reason.com/2025/12/31/study-short-form-video-isnt-rotting-your-brain); [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/s-is-all-that-scrolling-really-dulling-your-attention-researchers-combed-71-studies-the-link-is-real-but-how-compulsively-you-scroll-matters-more-than-how-long/)
- JMIR 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis on SFV use and mental health:
  - SFV use correlated with boredom (r = 0.42).
  - SFV addiction was positively associated with emotional suppression, alexithymia, emptiness and neuroticism — [JMIR 2026 e82503](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e82503/PDF)
- FOMO meta-analysis 2026 (16 studies, N=11,804): fear of missing out correlates with SFV addiction (pooled r = .494, 95% CI .38–.59). Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 97.8%), and the prediction interval ran from −.04 to .81 — [Int J Mental Health & Addiction 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-026-01762-9)
- Sleep:
  - Jiang & Yoo 2024 (1,629 Chinese high-school students, cross-sectional): more severe SFV-addiction symptoms were associated with poorer sleep quality, partly mediated by social anxiety. The design cannot show causation — [BMC Psychology 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-024-01865-9)
  - A JMIR systematic-review preprint (8 cross-sectional studies, N=9,060) concluded the evidence is insufficient to establish a robust, independent SFV–sleep association — [JMIR preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/114090)
  - Contrary finding: a 2025 *Frontiers in Public Health* study of Chinese children and adolescents reported short-video watching as a possible protective factor against insufficient sleep, varying by subgroup — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1644890/full)

**Internal industry evidence**
- Unredacted documents from Kentucky's 2024 lawsuit against TikTok (reported by NPR / Kentucky Public Radio, Oct 2024):
  - TikTok's own research reportedly found that users can form a habit after watching 260 videos.
  - The "35 minutes" figure is the state's own calculation from ~8-second clips, not a TikTok finding.
  - TikTok said the documents were cherry-picked and outdated — [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5150594); [KUNC/NPR](https://www.kunc.org/2024-10-11/lawsuit-documents-reveal-what-tiktok-executives-know-about-apps-effect-on-teenagers)

**Legal / regulatory context (only as it affects creators)**
- **US litigation:** on Mar 25, 2026 a Los Angeles jury (first JCCP bellwether, plaintiff "K.G.M.") found Meta and YouTube negligent in platform design and in failing to warn.
  - Damages: ~$3M compensatory (split 70% Meta / 30% YouTube) plus $3M punitive (≈$6M total).
  - TikTok and Snap settled before trial. Both defendants said they would appeal.
  - Google argued YouTube is "a streaming platform, not a social media site" — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/25/meta-youtube-los-angeles-california-verdict.html); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/verdict-reached-landmark-social-media-addiction-trial-rcna263421); [ABC7](https://abc7.com/post/los-angeles-social-media-addiction-trial-jury-finds-instagram-youtube-liable-landmark-court-case/18771272/)
- **US state law (New York SAFE for Kids Act):** final rules were released July 28, 2026 and take effect Jan 25, 2027.
  - Covered platforms must turn off personalized ("addictive") feeds and overnight (midnight–6am) notifications for under-18 users unless there is verifiable parental consent.
  - Age assurance is required, and penalties run up to $5,000 per violation — [NY AG press release](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-governor-hochul-release-final-safe-kids-act-rules); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/new-york-attorney-general-releases-final-rules-for-safe-for-kids-act)
- **EU:** on Feb 6, 2026 the European Commission preliminarily found TikTok's design in breach of the Digital Services Act.
  - The features named were infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and the personalized recommender, and the Commission said TikTok had not adequately assessed their risks to minors.
  - Remedies floated include disabling infinite scroll over time, effective screen-time breaks (including at night) and adapting the recommender.
  - The findings are preliminary, with no fine yet, and TikTok called them "categorically false" — [Euronews](https://euronews.com/2026/02/06/tiktoks-addictive-design-breaches-eu-law-commission-says); [SCL](https://www.scl.org/european-commission-says-tiktoks-addictive-design-breaches-dsa/)
- **EU, unconfirmed (single sources only):** a similar preliminary finding against Instagram/Facebook (July 10, 2026), and a proposed Digital Fairness Act with addictive-design rules (Nov 2026) — [Schjødt](https://schjodt.com/news/addictive-platform-design-under-dsa-scrutiny-the-european-commissions-preliminary-findings-on-tiktok); [Substack commentary](https://fragwyz.substack.com/p/the-digital-fairness-act-needs-a)
- **UK:** Ofcom's Protection of Children Codes under the Online Safety Act applied from 25 July 2025. Services with recommender systems that pose medium or high risk must configure algorithms to filter harmful content out of children's feeds, and age checks became mandatory — [Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/new-rules-for-a-safer-generation-of-children-online); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/whats-changing-for-children-on-social-media-from-25-july-2025)
- **Australia:** from 10 Dec 2025, platforms including YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Snapchat, Facebook, Reddit, X, Twitch, Kick and Threads must take reasonable steps to stop under-16s from having accounts. YouTube Kids is excluded — [eSafety FAQ](https://www.esafety.gov.au/about-us/industry-regulation/social-media-age-restrictions/faqs)
- **Australia, enforcement:** in March 2026 eSafety began investigating Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube over compliance. About half of surveyed under-16s still had a YouTube account. Penalties were raised in mid-2026, and the government claimed ~5M under-16 accounts had been removed — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/31/australian_social_media_ban_enforcement_report/); [Cyber Daily](https://www.cyberdaily.au/culture/13821-australia-doubles-social-media-ban-fines-as-esafety-gets-greater-powers)

### Inferences
- The creator's audience is genuinely heavy-using. About 1 in 5 US teens are "almost constantly" on TikTok or YouTube, so a meaningful slice of viewers will meet the content in a compulsive, low-attention scrolling state. This argues for content that is easy to process and delivers value fast, and against tactics that deepen compulsion.
- The best synthesis (Nguyen et al. 2025, plus the Reason/Silicon Canals critique) suggests harm associations track compulsive use intensity more than minutes watched. A creator cannot control a viewer's total time, but can avoid design choices whose main purpose is to extend compulsive viewing (fake cliffhangers, "part 2" bait, endless-loop traps).
- Regulation targets platform design features (infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications, recommenders), not creators. The indirect effects matter, though:
  - From Jan 2027, New York minors without parental consent may see non-personalized feeds.
  - Australian under-16s cannot hold accounts.
  - Both make subscriptions, follows and search discoverability relatively more valuable than algorithmic virality with teen viewers.
- YouTube is now a named defendant in a jury verdict about youth harm. Platforms are likely to keep adding teen friction (timers, bedtime takeovers). Content that "survives" a take-a-break prompt, i.e. that viewers actively seek out, is more durable than content that depends on uninterrupted scrolling. This is inference, not evidence.
- "Addiction" is contested language. The research mostly uses adapted scales (Bergen-style, TikTok Addiction Test) with arbitrary cutoffs. The brief should say "problematic" or "compulsive" use and give a range (≈4–18% depending on scale and sample) rather than a single "X% are addicted" headline.

### Gaps
- No primary-source meta-analysis pooling the *prevalence* of short-video addiction specifically was found. Only single studies and a 4–12% range cited by the Nature 2025 paper.
- No 2025–2026 Common Sense Media, eMarketer or DataReportal time-spent figures for TikTok/Shorts by Gen Z were retrieved. Gallup's 4.8 h/day is from 2023.
- Data on young adults 18–29 (as opposed to 13–17 teens) is thin in what was retrieved. Pew's adult platform-use data was not fetched.
- The exact Pew YouTube "almost constantly" percentage could not be verified because pewresearch.org was unreachable.
- The California SB 976 litigation status and the outcomes of later US bellwether trials were not found.
- It could not be confirmed whether YouTube's under-16 account restriction in Australia still lets logged-out teens watch Shorts.

## 2. Psychological mechanisms behind short-video engagement and "addiction"

### Takeaway
The best-supported mechanisms are curiosity and information gaps (which boost memory as well as engagement), novelty and surprise, personalization (which engages self-relevance networks in the brain), frictionless continuation (autoplay and infinite scroll, now the focus of regulators), and high-arousal emotion (which drives sharing). Several popular explanations are overstated or weak: "dopamine hits" as pleasure, the Zeigarnik "open loops are remembered better" claim, and treating any heavy use as clinical addiction. The peak-end rule is robust and directly useful for endings.

### Cited Findings

**Personalization and reward circuitry**
- Su et al. 2021, *NeuroImage* 237:118136 (fMRI, ~30 Chinese university students):
  - Personalized TikTok/Douyin videos produced higher activation than non-personalized ones in default mode network (DMN) subregions, the ventral tegmental area (VTA), lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior thalamus and cerebellum.
  - DMN regions coupled more strongly with audiovisual and frontoparietal networks.
  - The study does not measure dopamine or addiction directly. Press coverage overstated it as "reward center activated" — [PubMed 33951514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951514/); [Elite Daily (pop coverage)](https://elitedaily.com/news/how-tiktok-affects-your-brain-algorithm-personalized-videos)

**Dopamine claims (where pop science overstates)**
- Neuroscientific consensus summarized in reporting: dopamine does not itself cause pleasure. Pleasure can occur without dopamine and vice versa — [LitHub, "Exposing Myths About Dopamine"](https://lithub.com/a-complex-yet-crucial-chemical-exposing-myths-about-dopamine/)
- Dopamine release from social media, games and junk food is substantially lower than from addictive drugs (Khaleej Times, citing reward researcher Kent Berridge's critique) — [Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/world/we-have-a-dopamine-problem)
- "Dopamine detox" / "tolerance break" framing is not supported. Dopamine levels do not simply reset by abstaining, and a Harvard psychiatrist called the idea a fundamental misunderstanding. Breaks can still help for behavioral reasons — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/health/Is-Dopamine-Detoxing-Actually-Backed-by-Science.aspx)
- Dopamine is better described as a signal for salience and learning ("what to pay attention to or make effort for") than a "feel-good hormone". Social-media "dopamine addiction" is not a recognized DSM diagnosis — [Sabine Hossenfelder summary](https://rosetta.to/u/sabinehossenfelder/dopamine-addiction-is-a-myth-here-s-what-the-science-says)
- Counterpoint: routine pleasurable activities do involve dopamine systems, and constant stimulation does affect reward systems. The debate is about magnitude and the "addiction" label, not whether reward learning is involved — [RealClearInvestigations](https://www.realclearinvestigations.com/articles/2025/07/28/addiction_fiction_dopamine_is_not_why_kids_love_tiktok_1124276.html)

**Infinite feed, autoplay, frictionless swiping, intermittent reward**
- The European Commission's Feb 2026 preliminary DSA findings name infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and the personalized recommender as the "addictive design" features of concern for minors — [Euronews](https://euronews.com/2026/02/06/tiktoks-addictive-design-breaches-eu-law-commission-says); [Verfassungsblog analysis](https://verfassungsblog.de/tiktok-dsa/)
- TikTok internal research (via Kentucky lawsuit filings): habit formation after ~260 videos — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5150594)
- Context switching: Chiossi et al. 2023 (CHI '23; between-subjects lab study, N=60 German university participants) found that a TikTok session degraded prospective memory (remembering to carry out an intended action) on a later task. Twitter and YouTube sessions showed no significant effect. The authors attribute this to rapid context switching across short, unrelated videos — [arXiv 2302.03714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03714v1)

**Curiosity gap / information-gap theory**
- Loewenstein 1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98: curiosity arises from a perceived gap between what one knows and wants to know. A small amount of initial information widens the felt gap, which intensifies curiosity, and curiosity falls once enough is known — [Loewenstein 1994, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) (theory described via [Kang et al. PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf))
- Kang et al. 2009, *Psychological Science* ("The wick in the candle of learning"): curiosity about trivia questions activated reward-related regions, and higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1–2 weeks later — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, *Neuron*: in a curious state, people remembered the answers they were curious about better, and also incidental material shown during high curiosity, both immediately and one day later. The effect involved midbrain dopaminergic circuits and the hippocampus — [PMC4252494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)
- Caveat: later work questions whether some "curiosity" learning boosts are really driven by the partial prior knowledge that triggers curiosity — [Murphy et al. 2020](https://hmlpubs.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/214/2022/05/2020_Murphy.pdf)

**Zeigarnik effect / open loops (myth-adjacent)**
- Ghibellini & Meier 2025, *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*, meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects:
  - **No general memory advantage for unfinished tasks** (the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity).
  - There is a robust general tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect) — [IDEAS/RePEc listing, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- Wikipedia summarizes earlier failures to replicate Zeigarnik's recall difference — [Wikipedia: Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)

**Peak-end rule (for endings)**
- Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993, *JPSP* (film clips): peak and final affect together explained ~94% of variance in retrospective evaluations, while duration added only ~3% ("duration neglect") — [Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)
- 2022 meta-analysis in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* (174 effect sizes): strong support for the peak-end rule. The rule applies to experiences with a clear start and end, which short videos have — [IDEAS listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html); [Wikipedia: Peak–end rule](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Peak_end_rule)

**Social proof / FOMO**
- FOMO is strongly correlated with SFV addiction in a 2026 meta-analysis (r ≈ .49, extreme heterogeneity) — [Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-026-01762-9)

**High-arousal emotion, surprise, practical value (sharing)**
- See Section 4 (Berger & Milkman 2012).

### Inferences
- **Curiosity is the one "hook" mechanism with strong evidence that it also helps memory** (Kang 2009; Gruber 2014). An honest information-gap hook therefore serves both engagement and learning. That makes it the most ethical high-performing hook for a "useful tips" channel, provided the gap is closed within the same video.
- **"Open loops" work through curiosity and the tendency to resume, not through Zeigarnik memory.** The 2025 meta-analysis undermines the marketing claim that unresolved loops are "remembered better". The pull to resume interrupted things is real, though, and that is exactly what deceptive "part 2" or cliffhanger bait exploits. For minors, the ethical line is: open a loop only if it closes in the same video.
- **Personalization and endless feeds are platform levers, not creator levers.** A creator's controllable levers are clarity, novelty or surprise, humor, usefulness, and a satisfying ending.
- **Process-level framing for the brief:** short-form video is compelling because it combines rapid novelty sampling (each swipe is a cheap "lottery ticket" of unpredictable quality, the intermittent-reward analogy), personal relevance via the recommender, and zero friction to continue. The intermittent-reward analogy is a reasonable *descriptive* model, but no retrieved study directly quantifies TikTok's reward schedule. Flag it as a model, not a measured fact.
- **Dopamine language should be avoided or caveated** in the final report. Say "reward learning and salience" instead of "dopamine hits", and do not claim short videos "fry" dopamine receptors.
- **Peak-end implies the ending is disproportionately important** for how a viewer remembers and rates a video, and so for whether they follow. A crisp, satisfying payoff plus a light deadpan "button" at the end should beat padding.

### Gaps
- No primary study was retrieved that directly measures variable-ratio reinforcement in TikTok/Shorts swiping. The slot-machine analogy rests on behavioral-economics reasoning and internal documents, not on direct measurement.
- Processing fluency (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004, *PSPR* 8(4):364–382, "Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure") was not retrieved this session. It is listed from bibliographic knowledge only: easier-to-process stimuli are judged more pleasant and truthful. The report writer should treat it as background, not a verified finding here.
- No study was found on autoplay or swipe friction at the individual-video level, e.g. whether shorter videos are swiped less.

## 3. Attention spans and learning from short video

### Takeaway
The "8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish" claim is a myth with no peer-reviewed basis. Real research separates transient, sustained and selective attention. Evidence that short-video *use* is associated with poorer attention and inhibitory control is moderate in size but mostly correlational. Two experiments suggest that *fragmented, context-switching* short-clip viewing impairs memory compared with coherent content. For learning, the multimedia-learning research (signaling, segmenting, coherence) and the curiosity research give clear, evidence-based design guidance. A single coherent 30–60 s explainer is closer to "segmented coherent content" than to "fragmented feed".

### Cited Findings

**The goldfish myth**
- The "8 seconds" figure comes from a 2015 Microsoft Canada marketing report that sourced it to the website "Statistic Brain". It was traced to a 2008 web-browsing study (Weinreich et al.) that did not measure attention span. There is no evidence goldfish have 9-second attention or memory — [WARC, "The goldfish myth"](https://www.warc.com/NewsAndOpinion/opinion/The_goldfish_myth/2806); [Univ. of Wisconsin course note "Goldfish MythBusting"](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-09/Goldfish_MythBusting.pdf); [eLearning Industry](https://elearningindustry.com/8-second-attention-span-organizational-learning)
- A King's College London survey reportedly found ~50% of people wrongly believe the goldfish claim. Researchers distinguish transient attention (seconds-long orienting) from sustained or selective attention, which depends on the task and motivation — [Lenz claim analysis (secondary)](https://lenz.io/c/human-attention-span-shorter-than-goldfish-17c171c0); [BBC-linked "Busting the attention span myth"](https://www.downes.ca/post/74864/rd)

**Short-video use and attention (correlational)**
- Nguyen et al. 2025 (*Psych Bull*): attention r = −.38 and inhibitory control r = −.41 with SFV use. The cognitive subset was mainly Chinese cross-sectional studies, and the association was stronger with compulsive use than with duration — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/); [Reason critique](https://reason.com/2025/12/31/study-short-form-video-isnt-rotting-your-brain)

**Experiments on short-video viewing and memory**
- Chiossi et al. 2023 (CHI, N=60): a TikTok session impaired prospective memory, while Twitter and YouTube did not. The proposed cause is rapid context switching — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03714v1)
- Wei, Li, Ni, Li, Liu & Dong (Guang-Heng Dong lab), "Learning via short videos impairs memory accuracy and reduces brain synchrony" (college students; ~57–59 in the fMRI sample per conflicting reports; not preregistered per one report):
  - A documentary chopped into short clips produced lower immediate quiz scores than the continuous version (~44% vs ~66%), and more forgetting the next day.
  - It also produced lower inter-subject neural synchrony in episodic-memory, visual-attention and control regions.
  - Caveats: the stimuli were travel clips, and the effect is attributed to fragmentation and context switching, not brevity per se — [ScienceBlog analysis](https://scienceblog.com/t-short-form-videos-may-make-newly-learned-information-harder-to-remember-but-the-experiment-tested-fragmented-travel-clips-rather-than-every-brief-lesson-or-every-persons-scrolling-habit/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/brain-scans-reveal-how-short-videos-impair-memory-and-disrupt-neural-pathways/)

**Educational video length and format**
- Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014 (ACM Learning@Scale; 6.9M video-watching sessions across 4 edX courses):
  - Engagement drops sharply after ~6 minutes.
  - Khan-style tablet *drawing* tutorials were more engaging than PowerPoint slides or code screencasts.
  - Chopped-up classroom lectures were less engaging than video made for the medium — [Guo et al. PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf); [Rochester PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
- Short-form education studies (2024–2025) are small and often self-reported. One TikTok/YouTube Shorts study found higher post-test scores but framed short-form as a "gateway" to deeper learning rather than a replacement — [ResearchGate 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404706454_How_TikTok_Pedagogy_and_YouTube_Shorts_Are_Transforming_Student_Engagement_Conceptual_Learning_and_Educational_Motivation_in_Hybrid_Learning_Ecosystems); [Pharmacology short-video study (self-reported retention)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12752264/)

**Mayer multimedia-learning principles**
- **Signaling** (highlighting key words and structure): Schneider et al. 2018 meta-analysis (~103 studies / 145 comparisons, N=12,201) found g = 0.53 for retention and g = 0.33 for transfer — [Brom & Hannemann lecture slides summarizing it](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf); [ACU signalling summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling)
- Richter, Scheiter & Eitel 2016 meta-analysis: a positive signaling effect, strongest for learners with low prior knowledge — [ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling); [Alpizar et al. 2020 (moderators)](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)
- **Segmenting:** Rey et al. 2019, "A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect", *Educational Psychology Review*. The bibliographic record was found but its effect sizes were not retrieved — [Brom & Hannemann slides citing it](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Myth to flag in the final report:** "Gen Z has an 8-second attention span." The defensible claims are narrower:
  1. Viewers *decide* very fast whether to keep watching in a swipe feed. That is a selection decision, not a capacity limit.
  2. Compulsive short-video use is associated with weaker sustained attention and inhibitory control.
  3. Fragmented, rapidly switching content harms memory compared with coherent content.
- **A single-topic, coherently narrated sub-60 s video with a clear structure (Hook → Why → What → How → Solution) resembles a "segmented coherent lesson".** It does not resemble the "fragmented clip stream" that Wei et al. and Chiossi et al. found harmful. The channel's format is already on the right side of that evidence. Do not cut between unrelated micro-topics within one video.
- **Signaling has the strongest meta-analytic support for retention.** Short on-screen keywords that match the narration, arrows and highlights, and a visible step count ("1/3") are cheap in vector animation and evidence-backed. This matters most for novices, i.e. "boring but useful" tips aimed at people who don't know the trick yet.
- **Guo et al.'s finding that hand-drawn, Khan-style visuals beat slides** loosely supports simple, "being-drawn" vector animation over static text cards. This is extrapolated from long-form MOOCs to Shorts.
- **Coherence principle (inference, not retrieved here):** decorative motion, unrelated stock gags and background clutter that do not carry the information likely hurt learning. Keep visuals literal and minimal.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study directly compares a well-designed 45–60 s educational short with a 3–5 min version on delayed retention.
- Rey et al. 2019 segmenting effect sizes and Mayer's coherence/seductive-details meta-analyses (e.g. Rey 2012) were not retrieved. They are cited from bibliographic knowledge only.
- The Wei et al. paper's journal, year and exact N could not be verified (PsyPost was unreachable, and secondary reports conflict).
- No study on Gen Z-specific sustained attention change over time (cohort comparisons) was found.

## 4. Humor, memory and sharing (including deadpan; what makes people share; practical value)

### Takeaway
Humor that is relevant to the content and non-aggressive tends to help motivation and modestly help recall. Humor *woven into* the core explanation can distract and reduce learning, while humor placed *beside* the content (before or after key points) helps. Sharing is driven by high-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety) and by practical value, interest and surprise. Low-arousal content shares less, which is a real tension for a "calm, deadpan" channel. No research was found on deadpan specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **Berger & Milkman 2012**, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), "What Makes Online Content Viral?" (field data: ~7,000 New York Times articles; plus experiments):
  - Content evoking high-arousal positive emotion (awe) or high-arousal negative emotion (anger, anxiety) is more likely to be shared, and low-arousal emotion (sadness) less likely.
  - Positive content is shared more than negative overall.
  - The arousal effects hold when controlling for how surprising, interesting or practically useful content is. Practical value, interest and surprise were treated as separate predictors of sharing — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Northeastern copy](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf); [DOI 10.1509/jmr.10.0353](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/what-makes-online-content-viral/)
- **Humor in education, review of four decades** (as summarized by Edutopia): related, appropriate, non-aggressive humor improved retention, while cruel or unrelated humor did not. Humor can be appreciated without improving retention — [Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/blog/laughter-learning-humor-boosts-retention-sarah-henderson)
- **Bolkan, Griffin & Goodboy 2018**, *Communication Education* 67(2):144–164 (two student experiments, the second a replication):
  - *Integrated* humor, built into the explanation itself, may reduce learning, likely by distraction.
  - *Contiguous* humor, placed adjacent to core content, can be beneficial.
  - The authors advise caution with jokes used as the examples themselves — [NCA summary](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/using-humor-classroom-beneficial-or-detrimental-student-learning/); [T&F DOI 10.1080/03634523.2017.1413199](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03634523.2017.1413199)
- **Small video-humor studies:**
  - University video lectures with *related* humor outperformed related-neutral versions on motivation and recall, with differences in EEG beta/theta activity (within-subjects; small sample).
  - A 228-student multimedia experiment found instructional humor did not act as a "seductive detail". Humor was rated funnier, rated less interesting, and made learners more aware of what they had not learned — [Conscientia Beam article](https://archive.conscientiabeam.com/index.php/61/article/download/3006/6710); [UNIMAS repository](https://ir.unimas.my/id/eprint/49596/1/The%20Role%20of%20Related%20Humor.pdf)
- Vendor claims such as "humorous video more than doubled delayed recall" could not be verified against primary studies — [Video Arts blog (vendor)](https://www.videoarts.com/resources/blog/why-humour-makes-learning-stick-the-neuroscience-behind-laughter-and-learning/)

### Inferences
- **Deadpan placement:** use deadpan lines as *contiguous* humor. The best spots are:
  - the hook's framing ("This is the most boring video you'll watch today. It will save you $40."),
  - a button after the solution,
  - understated captions *beside* key information.

  Avoid making the explanation itself the joke (the integrated-humor risk). Keep the actual steps literal and clear.
- **The arousal tension.** Calm, deadpan delivery is low-arousal in tone, which Berger & Milkman's data predict is less shareable. Two ways to compensate without manipulation:
  1. Make the *content* produce high-arousal *positive* emotion: surprise, awe ("wait, that's been there the whole time?"), amusement at absurd but true facts.
  2. Maximize *practical value*, an independent predictor of sharing that suits "useful tips".

  The calm narrator plus a surprising, useful payload is a coherent mix: low-arousal delivery, high-interest content.
- **Do not chase the anger/anxiety route.** It works for virality (Berger & Milkman) but conflicts with the ethics brief for minors and invites rage-bait dynamics.
- **Odd facts and myth-busting** ("goldfish memory is a myth") combine surprise with social currency, i.e. something interesting to repeat to friends. Berger's later STEPPS framework (social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value, stories) fits this. It was *not* retrieved this session, so treat it as background.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research was found on *deadpan* or *absurdist* humor specifically, either for recall or for sharing among teens and young adults.
- No study was found on humor in sub-60 s vertical videos specifically. All the humor-learning evidence comes from classrooms or longer lectures.
- The Banas et al. (2011) four-decade review was seen only via Edutopia's summary. Primary effect sizes were not retrieved.

## 5. Counter-trends: fatigue, "brain rot" backlash, digital wellbeing, demand for calm content

### Takeaway
There is real cultural backlash: "brain rot" was Oxford's 2024 Word of the Year, platforms now push teen time limits and bedtime takeovers, and regulators are moving against addictive design. There is also measurable demand for soothing, relaxing and "slow" content among Gen Z. However, short-form use itself is not declining in the survey data. "Fatigue" is stronger in commentary than in usage numbers. A "calm in the chaos" niche is plausible as positioning *within* short-form, not as an alternative to it.

### Cited Findings
- **Oxford Word of the Year 2024: "brain rot"** (chosen after a public vote of 37,000+ people and lexicographer analysis). Usage frequency rose 230% from 2023 to 2024. Oxford defines it in terms of the overconsumption of trivial, low-quality online content. Earliest recorded use: Thoreau's *Walden* (1854) — [Oxford University Press](https://corp.oup.com/news/brain-rot-named-oxford-word-of-the-year-2024/)
- **TikTok wellbeing features:**
  - All under-18 accounts have a default 60-minute daily screen-time limit (teens can enter a passcode to continue unless a parent has set the limit).
  - Since March 2025, under-16s who use the app after 10pm get a full-screen "wind down" interruption with calming music, and a second, harder-to-dismiss prompt if they continue.
  - Meditation exercises were being tested in the reminder.
  - Family Pairing "Time Away" schedules let parents block access — [TikTok Newsroom (Mar 11, 2025)](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/new-ways-we-are-supporting-parents-and-helping-teens-build-balanced-digital-habits?lang=en); [TikTok UK Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-gb/new-ways-we-are-supporting-parents-and-helping-teens-build-balanced-digital-habits-uk); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/404074/tiktok-reminds-teens-to-wind-down-at-10-pm.html)
- **YouTube wellbeing features:**
  - Take a Break and Bedtime reminders are full-screen takeovers across Shorts and long-form, on by default for under-18s (Take a Break every 60 min; bedtime 10pm–6am, with a pre-bedtime reminder) (March 2025).
  - A user-set Shorts daily timer launched in Oct 2025.
  - From Jan 2026, parents of supervised teens can limit Shorts scrolling time, with an option to set it to zero announced — [YouTube blog: teen wellbeing](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/teen-wellbeing-on-youtube-europe/); [TechCrunch, Oct 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/22/youtube-adds-at-timer-for-you-to-stop-scrolling-shorts); [The Star, Jan 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/01/18/039take-a-break039-youtube-targets-the-endless-scrolling-of-teens)
- No independent evaluation was found showing these timers reduce teen use. One commentary calls the Shorts timer "a polite reminder in an app designed to keep you hooked" — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/apps/youtube-is-adding-a-timer-to-shorts-so-you-dont-scroll-the-day-away-185204383.html)
- **Demand for soothing / slow content:**
  - Think with Google / YouTube: 83% of Gen Z have used YouTube to watch soothing content that helps them relax. The source is a 2022 Ipsos survey, so this is not a 2025 figure. 69% say they often watch comforting creators or content, and Gen Z tunes into calm vlogs of cleaning, reading, gardening and drawing, often with calming music — [Think with Google, "Soothing videos"](https://business.google.com/ca-en/think/consumer-insights/gen-z-soothing-videos/); [YouTube Culture & Trends report](https://blog.youtube/culture-and-trends/culture-trends-report-gen-z-multiformat-shorts-creator-pop-culture/)
  - YouTube's September 2025 trends post notes a growing appetite for "slow TV": long-form, ambient and educational content — [YouTube Culture & Trends, Sept 2025](https://blog.youtube/culture-and-trends/trending-now-september-2025/)
- **Fatigue and preference signals (vendor surveys, moderate-to-low quality):**
  - Sprout Social says Gen Z is the only generation to prefer long-form video (>60 s) on YouTube, and the generation most likely to want more screen-time management tools for mental health (Q4 2025 Sprout Pulse Survey) — [Sprout Social](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/gen-z-social-media/)
  - "Slow content" / "scroll fatigue" feature articles exist but cite thin data — [PopWire 77 (low quality)](https://77wpp.com/gen-z-abandoning-short-form-video-longer-content-trend/)
- **Short-form remains dominant:** 92% of US Gen Z watch short-form frequently or sometimes (NuVoodoo Q1 2025), and daily TikTok/YouTube use among US teens is stable in Pew 2025 — [NuVoodoo](https://nuvoodoo.com/2025/04/04/new-data-short-form-video-explodes-in-popularity/); [Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/12/09/teens-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-2025/)
- **"Brain rot" backlash is partly contested in research commentary:** Reason argues the Psych Bulletin meta-analysis does not show that short video "rots" brains, given weak links to duration and cross-sectional data — [Reason](https://reason.com/2025/12/31/study-short-form-video-isnt-rotting-your-brain)

### Inferences
- **A "calm in the chaos" niche is plausible**, for four reasons:
  1. There is documented Gen Z demand for soothing and comfort content.
  2. A named cultural backlash ("brain rot") gives viewers a vocabulary for wanting something different.
  3. Platforms are adding friction that disadvantages content relying on uninterrupted bingeing.
  4. Faceless, deadpan, low-stimulation presentation is visually distinct in a high-stimulation feed, a contrast that can itself stop the scroll.
- **The niche is a positioning, not an escape from short-form mechanics.** The video still has to win the first 1–2 seconds in a swipe feed. Calm tone must pair with an immediate, concrete promise.
- **Teen viewers increasingly hit bedtime and take-a-break interruptions.** Videos that end with natural closure ("that's it, go fix your door") are compatible with those prompts. Formats that rely on "don't stop now" cliffhangers will be interrupted anyway and leave a worse peak-end memory.
- **Brand opportunity:** the creator can openly position the channel as "anti-brain-rot": useful in under a minute, complete in one video, no part 2 bait. Teens and parents who are aware of the discourse may value this. It is inference; no data was found on whether such positioning increases follows.

### Gaps
- No rigorous longitudinal data was found showing an actual *decline* in Gen Z short-form use or time spent, so "fatigue" is attitudinal and anecdotal.
- No data was found on the performance of "calm/deadpan" Shorts versus high-energy Shorts (retention, shares).
- The 83% soothing-content figure is from 2022. No 2025–2026 replication was found.

## 6. Translating the evidence into ethical hook, pacing and editing principles (incl. what platforms say about bait)

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence-based levers are also the ethical ones:
- an honest curiosity gap that is closed in the same video,
- early and frequent payoff,
- coherent, single-topic narrative,
- signaling with on-screen text,
- segmenting of steps,
- relevant humor placed beside (not inside) the explanation,
- practical value,
- a satisfying peak-end close.

Tactics that exploit compulsion or deceive (fake cliffhangers, "part 2" bait, misleading titles and thumbnails, rage-bait, fake urgency, like/follow bait, mass-produced repetitive AI content) are both ethically inappropriate for an audience with minors and increasingly penalized by YouTube and TikTok.

### Cited Findings (platform rules and evidence anchors)

**YouTube**
- The Spam, Deceptive Practices & Scams policy prohibits "misleading metadata or thumbnails": using the title, thumbnail or description to trick users into believing the content is something it is not. It applies to all content, including thumbnails and comments — [YouTube Help: Spam, deceptive practices and scams policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973)
- Engagement manipulation, e.g. offering rewards for likes, views or subscribers, or artificially inflating engagement, is a policy violation — [YouTube Help (spam policy)](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973)
- Dec 18, 2024: YouTube announced stronger enforcement against "egregious clickbait", where a title or thumbnail promises something the video does not deliver (example: "The president resigned!" with no resignation content), especially around news. It started in India, with removal initially without a strike — [Google India blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/)
- July 2025: YouTube Partner Program monetization guidance targets "mass-produced or repetitive" (inauthentic) content, which is relevant to templated, AI-narrated faceless channels. Coverage also reports that re-uploaded or duplicate Shorts lose reach in favor of originals — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/07/03/youtube-crackdown-repetitive-non-original-content/amp/); [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/youtube-ai-shorts-duplicate-content-460353); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/re-uploaded-shorts-lose-reach-as-youtube-favours-original-clips/)

**TikTok**
- Community Guidelines include separate "For You feed eligibility" standards. Content can remain on the platform but be ineligible for recommendation, and accounts that repeatedly post ineligible content can be made temporarily non-recommendable — [TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/tiktok-is-trying-to-clean-up-its-for-you-recommendations-210057825.html)
- Third-party summaries (not verified against TikTok's own text this session) say FYF-ineligible categories include:
  - unoriginal or low-quality reused content,
  - engagement bait such as "like for like" or "follow for follow",
  - fake rewards for gifts or follows (under Integrity & Authenticity).

  The current guidelines' effective date is reported inconsistently (Sept 2025 vs Sept 2026) — [ContentIQ summary](https://contentiq.media/rules/tiktok); [Shapeless AI notes](https://shapelessai.com/notes/unoriginal-content-rules-youtube-tiktok-instagram)

**Evidence anchors for hook and edit design (see Sections 2–4)**
- Curiosity gap → engagement *and* memory (Kang 2009; Gruber 2014) — [Gruber 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494); [Kang 2009](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- Unfinished loops do not get a memory advantage, but people do feel a pull to resume (Ghibellini & Meier 2025) — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- Signaling: g = 0.53 retention (Schneider et al. 2018) — [Brom & Hannemann slides](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)
- Fragmentation and context switching impair memory (Wei et al.; Chiossi et al. 2023) — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/t-short-form-videos-may-make-newly-learned-information-harder-to-remember-but-the-experiment-tested-fragmented-travel-clips-rather-than-every-brief-lesson-or-every-persons-scrolling-habit/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03714v1)
- Peak-end rule: endings dominate retrospective evaluation (Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993; 2022 meta-analysis) — [PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)
- Contiguous humor helps and integrated humor can hurt (Bolkan et al. 2018) — [NCA](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/using-humor-classroom-beneficial-or-detrimental-student-learning/)
- High-arousal positive emotion and practical value drive sharing (Berger & Milkman 2012) — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- FOMO strongly correlates with SFV addiction (r ≈ .49) — [Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-026-01762-9)
- Drawn, Khan-style visuals beat slides for engagement (Guo et al. 2014) — [PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)

### Inferences (practical principles; evidence strength noted)

**A. Hook types for the first ~1–2 seconds (all must be paid off in the same video)**
1. **Honest information-gap hook.** Name a concrete, mundane object plus a gap: "Your microwave has a button that isn't for popcorn." Strong evidence for curiosity driving engagement and memory. Ethical condition: the answer arrives in this video.
2. **Myth-bust / counterintuitive fact.** "Goldfish don't have 3-second memories." Surprise, a prediction error, gives high-arousal *positive* interest plus social currency. Moderate evidence (Berger & Milkman: surprise/interest; curiosity research).
3. **Specific practical-value promise.** "Unstick a zipper in 10 seconds with a pencil." Practical value predicts sharing. Specificity (time, object, outcome) makes the promise checkable, and therefore honest.
4. **Deadpan self-aware framing (contiguous humor).** "This is the least exciting thing you'll learn today. It will save you money." Contrast with the feed is the pattern interrupt. Evidence is moderate for relevant humor in general and absent for deadpan specifically.
5. **Visual-first hook.** The object or problem is on screen in frame 1, with a 3–6 word on-screen text that *matches* the spoken hook (signaling). Do not open on a logo or title card. This is a practitioner heuristic consistent with signaling and swipe-decision behavior.

**B. Pacing and structure (Hook → Why → What → How → Solution in <60 s)**
- **Early partial payoff:** state *what* the trick is by roughly the first third of the runtime. Use the remaining time for *how* and a demonstration rather than withholding the answer to the end. This reduces the "bait" feel and respects viewers in a low-attention state. It is inference from curiosity research plus ethical framing; no study tests the exact timing.
- **One coherent topic per video:** no mid-video jumps to unrelated facts (Wei et al. fragmentation; Chiossi context switching).
- **Segment the "How":** numbered steps with a visible counter ("1/3, 2/3, 3/3") and a brief visual beat between steps (segmenting principle; signaling).
- **On-screen text:** short keywords synchronized with narration, not full transcripts that duplicate speech. This follows signaling and Mayer's redundancy caution. Burned-in captions are still advisable for sound-off viewing, so treat it as a trade-off and keep them concise.
- **Visual change cadence:** change the visual when the *idea* changes. Calm, deadpan pacing can still have motion every few seconds, but avoid decorative motion that carries no information (coherence; Guo's drawn-explanation finding). The specific "cut every 2–3 s" rules circulating among creators were not found in peer-reviewed research and should be labeled as heuristics.
- **Humor placement:** deadpan asides *between* steps or as the closing button. Never make the instruction itself a joke (Bolkan et al. 2018).

**C. Endings (peak-end)**
- End on the highest-value moment: the solution visibly working, followed by a one-line deadpan button ("Anyway. Your door no longer screams.").
- A **natural loop** (the last line flows back into the first) is acceptable *only if* the video is complete and the loop is not designed to trick viewers into thinking they missed something. No research was retrieved on loop effects; this is an ethics-based judgment.
- Provide a **stopping cue** rather than a continuation cue, e.g. an off-screen action ("go try it"). This aligns with teen wellbeing features and the peak-end rule.
- Calls to action: a single, low-pressure follow line is fine ("Follow for more boring things that work"). Avoid conditional or coercive CTAs.

**D. Tactics to avoid with an audience that includes 13–17-year-olds** (ethical reasoning plus platform rules)
1. **Fake cliffhangers and withheld payoffs** ("wait for the end…" with no real payoff). This exploits the resume tendency (Ovsiankina) and leaves a worse peak-end memory. It also approaches YouTube's misleading-metadata policy if the title or thumbnail over-promises.
2. **Deceptive "Part 2" bait.** Splitting content that fits in one video, or promising a part 2 that never comes, exploits the resume pull and drives compulsive return. Some summaries list it as TikTok engagement bait.
3. **Misleading titles, thumbnails or first frames** that the video does not deliver. Explicitly prohibited (YouTube misleading metadata; "egregious clickbait" enforcement).
4. **Rage-bait / outrage framing and fear-mongering** ("You've been poisoning yourself every morning"). High-arousal anger and anxiety do increase sharing (Berger & Milkman), but exploiting them with minors is unethical and risks misinformation strikes.
5. **Fake urgency and FOMO** ("Watch before this gets deleted", "only 1% know this"). FOMO is strongly associated with problematic SFV use. The "only X% know" claims are usually fabricated statistics.
6. **Engagement bait:** "like if…", "comment X or bad luck", "follow for part 2", "like for like/follow for follow", or fake giveaways. Treated as engagement manipulation by YouTube and reportedly FYF-ineligible on TikTok.
7. **Insecurity or shame hooks** aimed at appearance, body or social status. These are inappropriate for teens and outside the channel's "useful tips" scope.
8. **Unverified "odd facts" generated by AI without checking.** Fabricated facts are deception, and "facts" channels are especially vulnerable. Verify every claim with a source.
9. **Mass-produced, templated AI output** with minimal original value. This risks YouTube's inauthentic/repetitive-content demonetization and TikTok's unoriginal-content FYF ineligibility. Add genuine curation, original scripts and original animation.
10. **Sensory-overload editing** that mimics fragmented feeds (rapid unrelated cuts, flashing). This harms comprehension (Wei; Chiossi) and contradicts the calm brand.
11. **Late-night re-engagement tactics** aimed at teens, e.g. "daily at midnight" series hooks. These run counter to platform bedtime protections and the NY SAFE for Kids overnight-notification logic.

**E. A five-question "ethical hook test" for each script** (derived from the above)
1. Is every promise in the title, thumbnail and first 2 s fully delivered in *this* video?
2. Would a viewer who rewatched feel informed rather than tricked?
3. Does the hook rely on curiosity, usefulness or surprise, rather than fear, anger, shame or FOMO?
4. Does the ending give closure and a stopping point rather than a "you must keep watching" pull?
5. Is every fact verified, and is AI generation disclosed where the platform requires it?

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests specific hook wordings, or the "first 1–3 seconds" retention cliff, in Shorts or TikTok. Platform analytics folklore (e.g. "70% retention at 3 s") was not verified and should not be presented as research.
- TikTok's own FYF-eligibility text on engagement bait could not be fetched and verified this session. Third-party summaries only.
- No study was found on the effect of seamless loops on rewatch rates or on viewer wellbeing.
- No research was found on how YouTube or TikTok recommenders treat "made for kids" versus general-audience content for 13–17 viewers. The brief should defer to the platform-policy researcher's notes for COPPA and "made for kids" settings.
- Whether Mayer's redundancy principle (on-screen text duplicating narration) holds for sound-off vertical video was not tested in any retrieved source.
